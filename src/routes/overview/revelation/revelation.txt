
# Revelation: The Day of the Lord

Revelation is hard because most readings treat its pieces as isolated units: a mark, a beast, a plague, a number. Once the whole is seen, each piece has a place. Nobody understands the entire book. The aim is not an infallible reading. It is a set of principles that hold the book together.

## Seven premises

**1. Revelation is the day of the Lord.**

Joel 2:1: blow the trumpet in Zion, for the day of the Lord is at hand. Malachi 4:5: Elijah is sent before the great and dreadful day of the Lord.

That day is not simply the second coming, and it is not twenty-four hours. It is the span in which God steps back into human history: the pre-advent judgment, the examination of the living church, the time of trouble, the coming of Christ — and it may run beyond that. Peter’s word still stands: a day with the Lord is as a thousand years. Humanity has had its time to parade and to harm. God’s time is coming.

That is what John means by “I was in the Spirit on the Lord’s day.” Not Saturday. Not Sunday. In the Spirit he was taken into that period.

**2.** The focus is the judgment of two kingdoms.

**3.** Part A — chapters 4 to 11 — is the judgment of Christ’s kingdom.

**4.** Part B — chapters 12 to 19 — is the judgment of the beast’s kingdom.

**5.** The seven seals are the record of Christ’s kingdom, opened and examined in the judgment.

**6.** Chapters 12 to 19 are the matching record of the beast’s kingdom.

**7.** Both sections tell the same history, from opposite sides.

Once that template is in place, a detail is no longer a stray fact. You know where it belongs.

## The story as it runs

**Christ’s kingdom (4–11).** The seals are the history of the church. Just before the seventh, the kingdom appears in its final form: the 144,000. After they are sealed come the seven trumpets — the seven last plagues. In chapter 11, Christ receives the kingdom.

**The beast’s kingdom (12–19).** Chapter 12 opens the other side: the dragon against the woman and the man-child; war on God’s people. There is background — the woman, the beast, the wilderness — but the book does not linger. It is about the day of the Lord. It moves quickly to the two beasts: the first, then the two-horned lamb-like beast. Together they bring the mark of the beast.

**What the mark does to the church.** The crisis does not only threaten. It wakes.

Chapter 13 is followed at once by chapter 14: an angel in mid-heaven, with the everlasting gospel, announcing that the hour of God’s judgment has come. The earth is split into two camps — sheep and goats, lamps with oil and lamps without.

This is the ten virgins. At midnight a cry is made. They arise and trim their lamps. The midnight cry is that awakening. The three angels fly. The gospel goes to all nations in a short space of time.

No one manufactures that moment. You cannot create Pentecost, or the cross, or the Day of Atonement. A man may announce that the mark has come and try, by himself, to raise the great awakening. In a few weeks he is weary, and the world is as it was. The issue was not the issue, because God had not made it.

The midnight cry does not come from a teacher on earth. It comes from heaven: *Behold, the bridegroom cometh.* It will be unmistakable. Then the preaching begins.

**Harvest, then plagues, then a pause.** Revelation 14: a sickle; the earth is harvested. Revelation 16: the plagues, in sequence.

In the middle of that sequence the narrative stops. God says, in effect: *Hold. I will show you the judgment of the woman — the great harlot. She receives special treatment. Here is the background.*

That is Revelation 17. That is where this study now stands.

## The pattern

| | Christ’s kingdom | Beast’s kingdom |
|---|---|---|
| Record examined | Seven seals | Chapters 12–19 |
| Final form | 144,000 sealed | Mark of the beast |
| Last work | Three angels; midnight cry; harvest | Final assault |
| End of this age | Trumpets / plagues; Christ receives the kingdom (11) | Beast judged; the woman judged (17–19) |

The day of the Lord is that whole stretch — not a date on a calendar, and not a pile of disconnected symbols. It is God’s day, in which the two kingdoms are brought into the open, judged, and finished.
